## 1. Summary

This project is a training presentation that teaches people how to recognize phishing emails and fake websites, understand the social engineering tactics attackers use, and respond safely. It includes real-world incidents, practical best practices, and a 5-question quiz with answers.

- Teach the warning signs of phishing emails and fake websites

## 4. Key Content

### 4.1 Red flags in a phishing email

- Lookalike sender domain (for example `paypa1` instead of `paypal`)
- Urgent threats such as "act within 24 hours"
- Generic greeting like "Dear Customer"
- Links that do not match the real website
- Unexpected attachments or login requests
- Spelling and grammar mistakes

### 4.2 Recognizing fake websites

- Read the full domain. `paypal.com.secure-login.xyz` is not `paypal.com`.
- A padlock only means the connection is encrypted, not that the site is honest.
- Hover over links to preview the real address before clicking.
- Be careful with unexpected pop-ups asking for passwords or card details.
- Open important sites from a bookmark or by typing the address yourself.

### 4.3 Social engineering tactics

| Tactic | How it works |
|---|---|
| Urgency | Pushes you to act before thinking |
| Authority | Pretends to be a boss, bank, IT team, or police |
| Fear | Threatens fines, legal action, or lost access |
| Greed / Reward | Offers prizes or refunds that are too good to be true |
| Familiarity | Uses real names or projects to build trust |
| Curiosity | Teases leaked photos, profile views, or unexpected files |

### 4.4 Real-world cases

- **RSA (2011):** a spear phishing email with a spreadsheet attachment led to the compromise of RSA SecurID data.
- **Google and Facebook (2013-2015):** fake invoices posing as a real hardware vendor led to payments of over $100 million.
- **Twitter (2020):** attackers phoned employees (vishing) to gain access to internal tools and took over high-profile accounts for a Bitcoin scam.

### 4.5 Best practices

1. Stop and think before clicking
2. Verify requests through another channel (call back on a known number)
3. Never share passwords, OTPs, or PINs
4. Enable multi-factor authentication
5. Use a password manager with a unique password per site
6. Keep the OS, browser, and antivirus updated
7. Report suspicious emails to IT or security
8. Do not open unexpected attachments

## 5. Quiz

1. An email from `support@amaz0n-help.com` asks you to confirm your order. Is it safe or phishing?
2. A website shows a padlock, so it must be legitimate. True or false?
3. Your "CEO" emails you to urgently buy gift cards. What is the first step?
4. Which URL is the real one: `paypal.com.login-check.xyz` or `paypal.com`?
5. What is the best protection if your password leaks?

**Answers:** 1. Phishing  2. False  3. Verify by phone  4. `paypal.com`  5. Multi-factor authentication

## 6. If You Clicked a Phishing Link

1. Disconnect from the network and change your passwords
2. Report it to your IT or security team immediately
3. Run an antivirus scan and watch your accounts for unusual activity

## 7. Key Takeaways

- Phishing attacks target people, not just technology.
- Attackers rely on emotion: urgency, fear, and curiosity.
- Verifying through a second channel stops most attacks.
- Multi-factor authentication limits the damage when a password is stolen.
- Reporting quickly reduces harm for everyone.

## 8. Conclusion

Most successful attacks begin with a single click. Training people to pause, verify, and report is one of the most effective and cheapest defenses an organization can have.
